Case study · July–August 2026

StoreTracker

A native iPhone app that logs every store visit on its own, so the numbers that measure my workday are finally accurate.

Role
Designer, developer and sole user
Scale
528 stores · ~200 automated tests · 3 connected components
Built with
Swift, SwiftUI, Core Location, Core Data, Google Apps Script, TypeScript
01

The problem

I cover a retail territory of more than 500 stores across Maryland. In field work, time on site is one of the main ways performance gets measured, but nothing recorded it accurately. Logging by hand meant stopping at every store, rounding times, and hoping I didn't miss one. The data that described my own workday was the least reliable data I had.

I didn't want a better way to log visits by hand. I wanted a record that kept itself.

02

What I built

The app runs in the background all day. When I walk into one of my stores it records the arrival; when I leave it records the departure and the time on site. Each visit syncs to a Google Sheet where I can analyze it. I never have to tap anything. The app presents the data four ways.

03

How I built it

Three parts, each with a single job.

  1. 1

    Geocoding tool · TypeScript

    Reads the store list from an Excel export and converts each address to coordinates. Addresses it can't locate go into a failure report and stay out of the app, so no store is tracked in the wrong place. A merge mode adds only new locations, which kept 131 stores' visit history intact as the catalog grew to 528.

  2. 2

    iPhone app · Swift and SwiftUI

    Holds the core logic. Business logic lives in a separate, fully tested layer apart from the screens, so every number the app shows is calculated and verified.

  3. 3

    Google Sheets webhook · Apps Script

    Receives visits from the phone and writes them to the spreadsheet. The data lands in a tool I already use every day, with no server to maintain.

I wrote a design spec for each feature before building it. I developed with AI coding tools, making the architecture decisions and directing and testing each piece myself.

04

The hard problems

  • iOS watches only 20 locations at a time. I needed 528.

    The app tracks my general location in a battery-efficient way and, as I move into a new area, swaps in the 20 closest stores. It covers the whole territory without running continuous GPS.

  • Driving past a store shouldn't count as a visit.

    A lock-screen notification lets me confirm or reject each arrival with one tap, and any stop under three minutes is discarded unless I've confirmed it.

  • No visit gets lost, even with no signal.

    Every visit is saved on the phone first. Sync is a separate step that retries until the spreadsheet confirms receipt. A dead zone just means the visit waits in line.

  • No duplicate rows, ever.

    Each visit has a unique ID, and the spreadsheet updates existing rows instead of appending. A visit sent twice still ends up as exactly one row.

  • Corrections are allowed, and they're visible.

    I can fix a time, move a visit to the right store in a crowded plaza, or add one the phone missed. Every edited or hand-entered visit is permanently flagged in the data.

  • Secure without a login.

    The phone never signs in to Google. Each request carries a secret token stored in the iPhone Keychain, and the spreadsheet rejects anything without it.

05

The result

An automatic, minute-by-minute record of my workday across the whole territory, in a spreadsheet I can analyze.
